Tectonic Synchronicity: Rhythmic Seismic Discharge within the Cotabato Trench Divergent Double Subduction System

1. Introduction to the Cotabato Trench Tectonic Framework

The Cotabato Trench represents an active and geodynamically complex subduction zone situated off the southwestern coast of Mindanao, delineating the northeastern margin of the Celebes Sea. Within the broader architecture of the Philippine Mobile Belt, this trench facilitates the eastward subduction of the Celebes Sea lithosphere—a component of the Sunda Plate—beneath the archipelago.

While subduction initiation is geologically recent, dating to the late Miocene or Pliocene (approximately 11–2.6 million years ago), the system is characterised by high-velocity oblique convergence and significant seismic potential. This document synthesises the principles of Divergent Double Subduction (DDS) theory with empirical observations of rhythmic aftershock sequences, suggesting that the system is governed by a highly structured, mathematically predictable discharge of tectonic stress.

2. Geodynamic Setting: Divergent Double Subduction (DDS)

2.1 Principles of Outward Dipping Systems

Divergent Double Subduction (DDS), or outward-dipping double-sided subduction, is a rare tectonic configuration where two parallel subduction zones with opposing polarities develop on a single oceanic plate. As the central plate is consumed on both margins, it eventually assumes an inverted "U" morphology. This geometry is a primary driver for the closure of oceanic basins and the subsequent "arc-arc" collision of overriding plates.

2.2 The Cotabato Context

The Cotabato Trench is a prominent bathymetric feature reaching water depths exceeding 5,700 metres. It operates as part of a western subduction chain alongside the Sulu and North Sulawesi Trenches. Crucial to its mechanical behaviour is the nature of the subducting lithosphere: the trench consumes Eocene-aged oceanic crust (approximately 40 Ma, identified by magnetic anomalies 18–20). The proximity of the Celebes Sea basin provides a constant supply of relatively buoyant, older crustal material, which influences the slab's resistance to penetration.

2.3 Slab Morphology and Convergence

The megathrust interface at the Cotabato Trench is an east-northeast-dipping structure. Convergence rates are exceptionally high, estimated between 70 and 100 mm per year. The evolution of this DDS system can be categorised into three distinct geodynamic stages:

* Initial Stage: Bipolar subduction initiates, forming parallel volcanic arcs and accretionary wedges with opposing vergence.
* Soft Collision: As the ocean basin closes, the inverted "U" slab traps mantle material beneath it. This "trapped mantle" inhibits further subduction, while the overriding plates meet and weld into a unified, rigid block.
* Detachment: The dense oceanic slab eventually breaks away from the welded crustal block, sinking into the mantle and triggering decompression melting and bimodal magmatism.

5. Synthesis: The Stick-Slip Ratcheting Mechanism

5.1 High-Rate Compression Effects

The convergence rate of 100 mm/year acts as a "tectonic vise," applying unyielding pressure to the subduction interface. This rapid loading ensures that the fault environment remains under constant, high-velocity compression, forcing the system into a discrete ratcheting cycle.

5.2 The Coupling Paradox and Fault Locking

Standard tomographic models for the Cotabato Trench suggest "weak to moderate coupling" with aseismic extensions. However, the observed rhythmic discharge provides empirical evidence of localized high-friction "asperities" or locking zones. While the broader slab may exhibit weak coupling, these asperities act as mechanical regulators, accumulating strain and releasing it in the synchronized "echoes" observed in the aftershock sequence.

5.3 Slab Tearing and Kinematic Analogs

Structural segmentation is a primary driver of rhythmic energy release. Using the Manila Trench as a kinematic analog (Hsu et al., 2025), we observe how slab tearing—evidenced by dip angles shifting from 40° to 80°—dominates regional kinematics. In the Cotabato system, the inverted "U" shape and trapped mantle material provide the necessary mechanical resistance to inhibit steady subduction. This resistance, combined with the "welding" of overriding plates during the soft collision stage, creates a unified block capable of generating a consistent, rhythmic pulse across hundreds of kilometres of the fault plane.

6. Historical Context and Hazard Implications

6.1 Record of Megathrust Events

The Cotabato Trench has a history of devastating seismicity, confirming its status as a high-strain environment:

* 1918 Celebes Sea Earthquake (M_s 8.3): A shallow megathrust event causing local tsunamis and coastal destruction.
* 1976 Moro Gulf Earthquake (M_w 8.1): One of the deadliest events in Philippine history, causing over 8,000 fatalities.
* 2023 Mindanao Earthquake (M_w 6.8): A destructive thrust event characterised by clustered, rhythmic aftershocks.

6.2 Tsunami and Secondary Hazards

The risk of seafloor displacement in the Moro Gulf remains extreme:

* Tsunami Run-up: Potential wave heights of 5–9 metres, particularly dangerous in enclosed bays like Illana Bay.
* Liquefaction: High vulnerability in Holocene deposits and coastal marshes of the Zamboanga and Sarangani Peninsulas.
* Seismic Triggering: Intense ground shaking poses a risk of remobilising loose material on volcanic slopes, potentially triggering lahars or secondary hazards at Mount Apo.

6.3 Concluding Summary

The observed rhythmic nature of current aftershocks indicates that despite tomographic suggestions of weak coupling, significant portions of the fault remain friction-locked. The systematic discharge of energy at mathematical intervals (3.5x, 7x, and 9.5x multipliers) confirms that the region is under immense, steady stress. Given the history of great earthquakes and the current evidence of accumulated elastic strain, the Cotabato Trench poses an imminent and continuing risk for a magnitude 8+ megathrust event.
